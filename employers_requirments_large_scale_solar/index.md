# Employers Requirements Large Scale Solar

## System Behaviour, Electrical Safety and Long Term Infrastructure Reliability

# Preface

Solar power plants form a central component of the future electricity system. Electrification of transport, heating and industry powered by renewable electricity has the potential to reduce total primary energy consumption by approximately 70 percent compared with fossil fuel based energy systems.

This reduction arises from the efficiency of electric machines, heat pumps and power electronic conversion when compared with combustion based energy processes.

The development, construction and operation of solar infrastructure relies on the dedication of engineers, technicians, electricians, manufacturers, installers, operators and researchers working across the global energy sector.

This document is written in recognition of the many professionals who contribute to that effort and who work tirelessly to develop and maintain solar power systems.

The purpose of this guidance is to translate practical engineering observations into structured considerations that may assist the preparation of Employers Requirements and technical specifications for photovoltaic power plants.

# Executive Overview

## Purpose of This Work

This guidance sets out a structured engineering framework for preparing Employer Requirements for large scale solar photovoltaic power plants. It is intended to support developers, investors, owners, designers, EPC contractors, technical advisers and operators who need to define what evidence should exist before a project moves from design into procurement, construction, commissioning or long term operation.

Large scale solar projects are not simply collections of modules, inverters, cables, transformers and switchgear. They are distributed electrical infrastructure systems. The DC array, low voltage AC collector system, medium voltage network, substation interface, earthing system, surge protection system, power electronic controls, communication system and commissioning evidence must be reviewed as connected parts of one operating plant.

The central purpose of this work is to require proof. A drawing, equipment datasheet or supplier statement is not enough by itself. A project should be able to demonstrate that its electrical design is coherent, coordinated, maintainable, safe and aligned with the real physical conditions under which it will operate for decades.

## Engineering Philosophy

The guidance is based on the view that utility scale solar design should be treated as infrastructure engineering rather than product assembly. The electrical behaviour of a large plant emerges from the interaction of many elements. Cable geometry, conductor population, current sharing, inverter switching, transformer behaviour, surge paths, earthing arrangements, insulation life, thermal grouping and protection settings can all influence risk.

The guidance therefore does not prescribe one preferred technology, supplier or topology. It asks whether the selected topology has been properly evidenced. A string inverter plant, central inverter plant, combiner based architecture or hybrid arrangement may each be valid if the designer can show how the system behaves under normal operation, abnormal operation, fault conditions, maintenance conditions and long term environmental exposure.

The work is deliberately organised into linked technical sections. Each section can be reviewed independently, but each topic remains connected to the whole plant. This allows the document to act both as a technical checklist and as a deeper reference system for future development.

## Single Line Diagrams and Evidence

A central principle of this guidance is that a single line diagram is not a complete design unless it is supported by validated studies, equipment ratings, protection settings, cable calculations, earthing philosophy, surge protection coordination, commissioning evidence and as built records.

A simplified diagram can hide major risk. Low voltage switchboards may be shown without fault levels. MCCBs may be shown only by current rating rather than breaking capacity, service capacity, trip unit and settings. Cable routes may appear acceptable until grouping, duct fill, crossing points, soil thermal conditions and termination temperature limits are considered. Earthing may be reduced to a symbol even though DC earthing, AC earthing, lightning protection and medium voltage substation earthing may interact under fault and surge conditions.

For this reason the guidance treats design information, studies and commissioning records as one evidence chain. If the evidence is fragmented, the design cannot be properly reviewed.

## Low Voltage AC Collector Systems

The low voltage AC collector system is treated as a critical infrastructure layer. In string inverter solar plants this layer may contain very large populations of inverter feeder conductors, switchboard sections, protective devices, busbars, cable terminations, auxiliary supplies and transformer interfaces.

The guidance requires the low voltage AC topology to be fully defined from each inverter output to the relevant switchboard, transformer low voltage winding and onward medium voltage interface. It requires busbar ratings, prospective fault levels, breaker ratings, service breaking capacities, making capacities, trip settings, protection coordination, short circuit studies, cable thermal verification, earthing and neutral definition, harmonic assessment, isolation philosophy and commissioning evidence.

This is important because low voltage AC systems can be underestimated in solar projects. Individual inverter circuits may look modest, but the combined plant creates distributed current carrying conductor populations, thermal grouping, harmonic propagation, electromagnetic coupling, common mode behaviour and fault coordination requirements. The guidance therefore treats the low voltage AC system as a bankability issue, not a secondary detail.

## DC String and DC Cable Systems

The DC section is a core part of the guidance. It treats the photovoltaic DC field as a live outdoor electrical network with its own voltage stress, current behaviour, connector population, cable ageing, insulation life, leakage risk, capacitance, inductance, transient behaviour, surge exposure, earthing interaction and maintenance burden.

The guidance requires traceable string mapping from module to inverter input. It requires cold voltage verification, operating voltage checks, module current assessment, bifacial gain assumptions, short circuit modelling, cable rating verification, connector compatibility evidence, reverse current assessment, duct congestion review, thermal pinch point analysis, Class Two insulation maintenance, DC leakage assessment, floating system monitoring and surge protection coordination.

This section recognises that the DC side remains energised whenever sufficient sunlight is present. The DC array cannot be treated as passive cabling behind the inverter. Long cable routes, large connector populations, exposed insulation systems, module frames, mounting structures, junction boxes, surge devices, filters and inverter inputs together form a distributed outdoor electrical system.

## Materials, Insulation and Environmental Ageing

The guidance gives particular attention to cable material life and insulation integrity. PV DC cables, connector tails, module leads and exposed string wiring must maintain electrical insulation and mechanical integrity over the operating life of the asset.

The guidance therefore asks for evidence of ultraviolet resistance, weathering resistance, ozone resistance, damp heat performance, cold bending performance, cold impact performance, shrinkage behaviour, cross linked material performance, compatibility with connectors and suitability for the real installation environment.

This matters because insulation failure is not merely a cable defect. Cracking, shrink back, abrasion, water ingress or connector degradation can expose live DC conductors, create leakage paths, increase tracking risk, compromise Class Two separation, accelerate corrosion of metallic structures and weaken earthing integrity.

## Earthing, Leakage and Corrosion

Earthing is treated as a system issue rather than a drawing symbol. Solar plants may include DC array bonding, AC protective earthing, lightning protection earthing, medium voltage substation earthing, transformer neutral treatment, surge protection discharge paths, metallic mounting structures, cable containment and buried conductive infrastructure.

The guidance requires the designer to show how these systems interact under normal operation, insulation deterioration, surge events, lightning events, medium voltage earth faults, transformer faults and substation failure conditions.

DC leakage is treated as a long term infrastructure risk. Low level leakage may not operate conventional protection, but it can enter metallic frames, mounting structures, bonding conductors, earth connections and buried metallic infrastructure. Over time this can contribute to corrosion, increased contact resistance, reduced earthing effectiveness, dangerous touch voltage, transferred voltage or earth potential rise under abnormal conditions.

## Surge Protection, Transients and Power Electronics

The guidance treats surge protection as a coordinated system rather than a component selection exercise. Surge protection devices on the DC side, AC side and sensitive equipment interfaces should be selected and coordinated with earthing, bonding, lightning exposure, cable route geometry, inverter input protection and the wider plant architecture.

The document also recognises that large PV arrays contain capacitance, inductance and transient energy behaviour. DC circuits are not pure static conductors. Cable loop area, separated conductors, inverter switching, common mode voltage, MPPT operation, field collapse, filters, clamping devices and surge paths can influence voltage stress and electromagnetic behaviour.

The guidance therefore requires the designer to state how transient overvoltage, switching stress, insulation stress, DC side electromagnetic behaviour and common mode behaviour are controlled.

## Inverters, Harmonics and Model Validity

Modern solar plants depend on power electronic conversion. Transformerless inverter architectures can introduce coupling between the DC array and AC system through switching stages, filters, parasitic capacitance and common mode paths.

The guidance therefore requires a DC to AC interaction statement where transformerless inverters are used. It asks for review of leakage current, ground fault monitoring, common mode voltage, AC cable insulation, transformer low voltage winding withstand, secondary equipment withstand and residual DC current risk.

The guidance also states that a manufacturer power system model should not be accepted as a substitute for every electrical study. A model used for load flow, voltage control, reactive power control, frequency response or fault ride through should not be treated as a harmonic study unless the model provider confirms that it is valid for harmonic impedance, resonance and distortion analysis.

## Duct Congestion, Thermal Pinch Points and Losses

Cable sizing should not be based only on nominal current. The guidance requires thermal calculations to reflect the actual installation, including grouping, ducts, conduits, trenches, trays, soil thermal conditions, ambient temperature, crossing points, shared thermal environments and termination temperature limits.

The DC duct congestion section recognises that a high population of low current DC string circuits can create significant aggregate thermal load when grouped in ducts, trenches, containment bottlenecks, combiner entries or inverter input areas. Cable crossings between DC, low voltage AC and medium voltage circuits should be treated as local thermal pinch points.

Voltage drop and loss calculations are included because lifetime energy loss can materially affect project value. Electrical design is therefore linked to safety, reliability and commercial performance.

## Commissioning and Long Term Operation

The guidance requires design evidence to continue through construction, commissioning and operation. Required records may include string schedules, cable schedules, polarity test records, insulation resistance records, open circuit voltage records, string current records, connector inspection evidence, thermographic inspection, protection settings, as built drawings, earthing verification and commissioning procedures.

This is important because many project risks only become visible when drawings, calculations, installation records and commissioning evidence are compared. A plant may appear complete physically while still lacking the evidence required to prove that the electrical system is safe, coordinated and maintainable.
