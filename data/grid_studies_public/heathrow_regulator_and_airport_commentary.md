# Heathrow Regulator and Airport Commentary

Disclaimer: AI generated at Ventus Ltd request for GlobalGrid2050.com mission review.

The latest public position is that the Heathrow outage is now being treated as a **critical infrastructure resilience case**, not just a local substation fire.

The regulator view is centred on Ofgem’s investigation into National Grid Electricity Transmission after the NESO final report concluded that the North Hyde fire was most likely caused by a high voltage bushing failure linked to moisture ingress. Public reporting says an elevated moisture reading had been detected in July 2018, but appropriate mitigating action was not implemented. Ofgem described the issue as a preventable technical fault and opened an enforcement investigation into whether National Grid complied with relevant legislation and licence conditions for developing and maintaining the electricity system at North Hyde. ([reuters.com](https://www.reuters.com/business/energy/fire-that-led-heathrow-shutdown-caused-by-substation-component-failure-energy-2025-07-02/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The key regulatory escalation is the **independent audit of National Grid critical assets**. Ofgem is not only asking what happened at North Hyde. It is asking whether the failings identified by NESO were isolated or more systemic across the National Grid estate. That is the important shift. The incident is now a test of asset management culture, maintenance controls, risk escalation, fire suppression readiness and critical infrastructure dependency mapping. Ofgem’s public stance is that energy companies are expected to properly maintain networks and equipment, and that action will follow where evidence shows they have not. ([theguardian.com](https://www.theguardian.com/uk-news/2025/jul/02/heathrow-substation-fire-caused-by-fault-first-identified-seven-years-ago?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

NESO’s final technical position is severe. The fire was linked to catastrophic failure of a transformer high voltage bushing at the 275 kilovolt North Hyde substation. The likely mechanism was moisture entering the bushing, causing an electrical fault. NESO also found that the fire suppression system at North Hyde had been identified as inoperable before the incident. A 2022 National Grid review reportedly indicated that the suppression system was not working, and a July 2024 fire risk assessment said that if a fire started in one of the 3 supergrid transformers, it would not be suitably suppressed. That action was still outstanding at the time of the March 2025 fire. ([theguardian.com](https://www.theguardian.com/business/live/2025/jul/02/heathrow-fire-caused-preventable-fault-report-ofgem-launches-investigation-incident-tariffs-japan-business-live?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

From a GlobalGrid2050 perspective, the technical lesson is clear: a single component fault was allowed to become a national infrastructure event because of the interaction between asset condition, maintenance governance, fire suppression, network dependency and airport electrical architecture. The transformer bushing was the initiating component, but the system failure was broader. The incident disrupted Heathrow, caused major aviation losses, affected local power customers and exposed the weakness of assuming that critical infrastructure resilience is guaranteed simply because there are multiple supply points.

National Grid’s latest public response, as reported, is that it had a comprehensive inspection and maintenance programme and that it has taken further action since the fire. The specific measures reported include an end to end review of oil sampling processes, enhanced fire risk assessments across operational sites and retesting the resilience of substations serving strategic infrastructure. National Grid also said there are important lessons around cross sector resilience and the need for greater coordination. ([theguardian.com](https://www.theguardian.com/uk-news/2025/jul/02/heathrow-substation-fire-caused-by-fault-first-identified-seven-years-ago?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

That response matters because it moves the issue from one failed bushing to a broader asset intelligence problem. Oil sampling is only useful if abnormal results trigger action. Fire risk assessments are only useful if high priority actions are closed. Resilience testing is only useful if the customer’s private network consequences are understood. The North Hyde case suggests that maintenance data, operational risk and critical customer dependency were not fully joined into one live risk picture.

Heathrow’s commentary has 2 layers. First, the airport’s own Ruth Kelly review concluded that Heathrow made the right operational decision on the day. The review said shutting the airport was necessary for safety and that alternative choices would not have materially changed the outcome. Reuters reported that the review made 28 recommendations, including improving coordination with power distributor SSEN, improving monitoring of critical safety systems and assessing whether additional backup generation could improve resilience. ([reuters.com](https://www.reuters.com/world/uk/londons-heathrow-airport-responded-well-outage-march-review-finds-2025-05-28/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Second, Heathrow’s later response to NESO’s final report was much sharper towards the external electricity supply chain. Heathrow said the incident highlighted shortcomings in National Grid’s asset management and outdated safety standards. Public reporting also says Heathrow blamed a combination of outdated regulation, inadequate safety mechanisms and National Grid’s failure to maintain infrastructure. Heathrow has said work is already under way to implement the Kelly Review recommendations. ([theguardian.com](https://www.theguardian.com/business/live/2025/jul/02/heathrow-fire-caused-preventable-fault-report-ofgem-launches-investigation-incident-tariffs-japan-business-live?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The Heathrow internal resilience issue is still important. The Kelly Review reportedly found that Heathrow’s electricity system is around 75 years old in design terms and that losing one intake could disrupt operations for at least 8 hours while systems were reconfigured to accept power from the other 2 connection points. The review said there was no immediate fix and that Heathrow should work with airlines and the Civil Aviation Authority on future energy investment. Heathrow’s chief executive said a more resilient power supply system could cost around £1 billion. ([theguardian.com](https://www.theguardian.com/uk-news/2025/may/28/heathrow-ceo-thomas-woldbye-apologises-sleeping-power-outage?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

There was also a crisis management lesson. Heathrow’s CEO apologised after being unreachable in the early hours because his phone was on silent. The Kelly Review still concluded that the operational decision to close was correctly made by the COO, but recommended better critical incident notification processes, including alternative ways to contact senior leaders. This is not just a reputational issue. It is a resilience design issue. Critical infrastructure needs technical redundancy, communication redundancy and decision authority redundancy. ([theguardian.com](https://www.theguardian.com/uk-news/2025/may/28/heathrow-ceo-thomas-woldbye-apologises-sleeping-power-outage?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The latest combined message from regulator and Heathrow is therefore this: North Hyde was not merely a freak fire. It exposed weaknesses in maintenance follow through, transformer condition monitoring, fire suppression readiness, strategic substation resilience, airport internal distribution design, crisis notification and cross sector coordination.

For GlobalGrid2050, this case belongs beside Iberia, Texas, California wildfire, New York cascading failures and Waratah. It is the UK example of **critical infrastructure dependency failure**. A grid asset defect affected aviation, local consumers, emergency services, airline economics and public confidence. The regulator is now testing whether the issue was isolated or systemic. Heathrow is implementing internal resilience recommendations while pressing the external grid supply chain to improve. National Grid says it is reviewing oil sampling, fire risk and substations serving strategic infrastructure.

The deeper lesson is that resilience cannot be assumed at the boundary between public grid and private infrastructure. The supply point is not the whole answer. You must understand the path from transformer bushing, oil sample and fire suppression system all the way to airport terminal systems, airfield lighting, safety systems, passenger operations, backup generation and restoration sequence. This is exactly the kind of relationship GlobalGrid2050 should catalogue: not just assets on a map, but dependency chains that decide whether a fault remains local or becomes national disruption.
